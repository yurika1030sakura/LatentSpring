\title{LatentSpring: Transferable Physical Corrections for Molecular Generation from Atomic Composition}
\author{Anonymous}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Generating a molecule from atomic composition leaves both its connectivity and
three-dimensional geometry to be determined. LatentSpring combines a normalized
harmonic-mixture source with learned physical correction. A random auxiliary tree
organizes Gaussian coordinate displacements using atomic length scales, requiring
no known chemical bonds. A small equivariant head learns force-driven changes to
a frozen generator's predicted endpoints. Expressing these changes in coordinate
space lets the same head act on flow velocities or diffusion noise predictions.
On 64 new compositions and five independently trained EGNN fit pairs, physical
correction raises the fraction of outputs that are graph-valid and have GFN2-xTB
force RMS at most 5 eV/\AA\ from 7.21\% to 24.00\% for FM and from 8.75\%
to 25.37\% for GAGA. Three new fit pairs confirm improvement in every case.
Transferring the FM-trained head verbatim to GAGA improves this joint yield by
16.54 percentage points across those three pairs, without head retraining or
target-generator force labels. A separate source comparison improves graph
validity by 3.89 points on average across five fine-tuning runs. An optional
conditional hydrogen flow further improves physical geometry. Generation uses
neural evaluations, with no energy calls or quantum geometry optimization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
An atomic composition tells us which atoms a molecule contains, but not how they
are connected or arranged in three dimensions. We study generation from this
input, together with the molecule's total charge and spin multiplicity. The model
returns atomic coordinates. We then infer bonds from those coordinates to evaluate
the generated structure. Unlike conformation generation with a known molecular
graph, this task leaves both connectivity and geometry to be determined.

Flow matching learns to transform samples from a starting, or \emph{source},
distribution into molecular structures \citep{lipman2023flowmatching}. An isotropic
Gaussian is a simple choice, but it places all atoms on a common spatial scale
without using atom-specific geometric information. Harmonic sources instead
correlate atom positions through virtual springs. Existing graph-conditioned
methods can construct these springs from known chemical bonds. The question here
is how to obtain a useful structured source when those bonds are not given.

LatentSpring uses a random tree to organize the starting coordinates. We sample
Gaussian displacements along its edges, with scales determined by the connected
atoms' radii, and obtain atom positions by adding these displacements along the
tree. A new tree is drawn for every sample. Its edges are not proposed chemical
bonds: the tree is used only for initialization, and the flow can generate a
structure with different connectivity, including rings. Averaging over the trees
gives a normalized mixture of harmonic networks. We combine this source with an
equivariant coordinate network and geometry self-conditioning: the network makes
a provisional geometry prediction, then uses it to refine its velocity prediction.

Learning physical geometry poses a second challenge when the chemical graph is
unknown. An atomic potential can provide forces directly from coordinates, but
evaluating it at every sampling step adds repeated cost. We learn a small
correction from these forces offline. The frozen parent predicts a provisional
final geometry, and an energy model supplies
forces at that geometry during training. A bounded equivariant head learns the
additional velocity needed to shift the prediction along those forces. At
generation time, only the neural networks are evaluated. Expressing the head's
action as a change to the provisional endpoint also allows it to correct a
diffusion model's noise prediction. A complementary implementation transfers
physical feedback into the backbone weights by subtracting a matched
\emph{replay} update from an update trained on force-shifted structures.

We study two linked design choices: a normalized harmonic-mixture source that
requires no known chemical bonds, and learned physical changes to a generator's
predicted endpoints. The endpoint formulation makes the correction applicable to
both velocity and noise prediction. Controlled source comparisons and independent
backbones test these choices, while unseen compositions and independent physical
evaluation measure the quality of the generated coordinates.

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Molecular flows and source distributions.}
ConfGF and GeoDiff generate conformations for a supplied chemical graph
\citep{shi2021confgf,xu2022geodiff}. We use FlowMol3's equivariant coordinate
network and geometry self-conditioning modules \citep{flowmol3}. Equivariance
means that rotating the input rotates the predicted vectors, while relabeling
identical atoms relabels their outputs. HarmonicFlow combines harmonic sources
with self-conditioned flow matching for ligand docking; FlowSite extends this
approach to binding-site design \citep{stark2024harmonicflow}. ET-Flow also builds
a harmonic source from a supplied molecular graph \citep{etflow}. These methods
know the chemical connectivity at the input. Our source instead averages over
auxiliary spatial trees and requires only atomic composition. Other approaches to
source adaptation include guidance with a frozen vector field
\citep{wang2026sourceguided} and learned condition-dependent sources
\citep{kim2026bettersource}.

\paragraph{Distributions over trees.}
Factored tree distributions allow tractable probabilities and learning over trees
\citep{meila2000trees,duan2023spanning}. Gaussian mixtures defined through
random spanning-tree differences have also been used for imaging inverse problems
\citep{everink2026random}. We use isotropic edge displacements and atomic length
scales to define a distribution over centered molecular coordinates. Our source
experiments compare this mixture with isotropic Gaussian, shell-tree, and
covariance-matched Gaussian alternatives.

\paragraph{Physical feedback and nonequilibrium work.}
OMol25 provides molecular structures, energies, and forces across electronic
conditions \citep{omol25}. Energy-weighted flow matching
\citep{dern2025energyweighted}, importance-sampling-based training
\citep{fab}, and physical-reward fine-tuning of molecular diffusion models
\citep{zhou2025rlpf} provide ways to incorporate physical information into
generation. Simulation data also support protein fine-tuning \citep{lu2024fusing},
and physical proxies can guide residual correction of frozen video flow fields
\citep{wang2026pila}. FLEXDOCK regularizes predicted docking endpoints with a
geometry-based potential \citep{corso2025flexdock}. ConfDiff learns a force-guidance
network for protein conformations \citep{wang2024confdiff}, while
\citet{lai2026forceguidance} apply MMFF94 guidance during molecular flow and
diffusion sampling. We learn bounded equivariant corrections from bond-free
forces at generated provisional endpoints, keeping the parent fixed and using
neural evaluations during generation.
A separate extension uses nonequilibrium work identities to reweight local
physical targets \citep{jarzynski1997master,hummerszabo2001,escorted2008,jarzynski2002targeted}.
Related learned escorting supports multistate free-energy estimation
\citep{holdijk2026escorted}. We test local distribution recovery with controlled
molecular rotors and subsequent neural training.

\section{A harmonic source with latent connectivity}
The input is $c=((z_i)_{i=1}^{N},Q,S)$: the atomic number $z_i$ of every atom,
total charge $Q$ in elementary-charge units, and spin multiplicity $S$. Repeated
elements appear once per atom. A neutral singlet has $Q=0$ and $S=1$. For example,
$\mathrm{C_2H_6O}$ specifies two carbon atoms, six hydrogens, and one oxygen, but
allows different bond connectivities. Bonds are inferred from coordinates for
evaluation, not supplied as training labels.

The output coordinates form $X\in\RR^{N\times3}$, where row $x_i$ is atom $i$'s
position in angstroms. We remove absolute translation by subtracting the mean
position. The coordinates therefore lie in
$\HH_N=\{X:\sum_i x_i=0\}$, a space with $3(N-1)$ independent coordinates.

\paragraph{Sampling a tree.}
We first sample a spanning tree $T$ over the atoms: a connected graph with $N-1$
edges and no cycles. This tree tells us how to place the starting coordinates; it
does not specify the final chemical bonds. Each possible edge $(i,j)$ has a
positive weight, or affinity, $a_{ij}=a_{ji}$. We sample a tree with probability
proportional to the product of its edge weights:
\begin{equation}
 p(T\mid c)=\frac{\prod_{(i,j)\in T}a_{ij}}{\tau(a)},\qquad
 \tau(a)=\sum_T\prod_{(i,j)\in T}a_{ij}.
 \label{eq:tree-law}
\end{equation}
The sum $\tau(a)$ normalizes this distribution over all spanning trees. We use
$a_{ij}=u_i u_j$, where $u_i=0.05$ for H and F/Cl/Br/I, and
$u_i=\max(1,\text{default valence}(z_i)-1)$ otherwise. Larger $u_i$ favors more
tree edges incident to atom $i$. Small weights for hydrogen and halogens favor
terminal positions. Default valence is an element-level bonding capacity,
counting bond order; these weights require no molecule-specific bonds.

\paragraph{Placing atoms along the tree.}
For each tree edge, we independently sample a three-dimensional Gaussian
displacement. Its scale depends on $\ell_{ij}$, the sum of the two atoms' covalent
radii, which estimates a typical single-bond separation. We set
$\sigma_{ij}^2=\ell_{ij}^2\exp(s^2)/3$ with $s=0.2$. The edge displacement
$d\in\RR^3$ has density
\begin{equation}
 h_{ij}(d)=(2\pi\sigma_{ij}^2)^{-3/2}
 \exp\!\left[-\frac{\|d\|^2}{2\sigma_{ij}^2}\right].
 \label{eq:edge-law}
\end{equation}
Here $\sigma_{ij}$ is the standard deviation along each axis. All directions are
equally likely, and the radius-based scale determines the typical separation.
This choice also matches the edge covariance of the lognormal shell control.
The FlowMol implementation samples trees with weighted Wilson walks; the shared
EGNN uses weighted Pr\"ufer sequences for the same factorized tree law. We add
edge vectors along tree paths and subtract the mean position. We draw a new tree
for every sample. The resulting law is unchanged by rotations or relabelings of
identical atoms.

\paragraph{The source density.}
The tree is unobserved after initialization. Summing over it gives the source
density $q_0(X\mid c)$, a mixture of harmonic networks:
\begin{proposition}[Normalized source density]
For normalized isotropic edge densities,
\begin{equation}
 q_0(X\mid c)=N^{3/2}
 \frac{\tau\big(a_{ij}h_{ij}(x_i-x_j)\big)}{\tau(a)}
 \label{eq:source}
\end{equation}
integrates to one over the centered coordinate space $\HH_N$.
\end{proposition}
The numerator sums tree weights after multiplying each edge affinity by its
displacement density. The factor $N^{3/2}$ converts independent edge vectors into
centered atom coordinates. The matrix-tree theorem evaluates each sum through a
determinant rather than enumerating trees; Appendix~\ref{app:proof} derives the
normalization. Training only needs samples from the tree-and-edge procedure. It
does not evaluate this determinant. Physical learning uses a separate local
Gaussian neighborhood around each predicted endpoint.

We call the source \emph{harmonic} because each Gaussian edge density corresponds
to a quadratic spring energy. These virtual springs organize the initial random
coordinates; the learned flow determines the final molecular geometry.

\section{Integration with molecular flow matching}\label{sec:flow}
Given $c$, the flow transforms $X_0\sim q_0(\cdot\mid c)$ into
$X_{\rm gen}\in\HH_N$. The network takes the current coordinates $X_t$, flow
time $t$, and $c$, and predicts $v_\theta(X_t,t;c)\in\RR^{N\times3}$: one
velocity vector per atom. Integrating this field from $t=0$ to $t=1$ produces
the coordinates. The latent tree is used only to sample $X_0$ and is not passed
to the network as a bond graph. Energy and force evaluations are used during
physical training, not during generation.

\paragraph{Training pairs and velocity targets.}
We draw $X_0$ from the harmonic source and take a reference structure $X_1$ from
OMol25. We align the structures by matching atoms of the same type and rotating
them to reduce their coordinate displacement. We then apply the same random
rotation and the same random relabeling of identical atoms to both structures.
The rotation is uniform over orientations. This shared augmentation preserves
the source marginal (Appendix~\ref{app:symmetry}).

We use the linear path $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1$, with velocity target
$V^*=X_1-X_0$. Thus training is supervised regression from an intermediate
structure to the displacement between its paired endpoints. The variable
$t\in[0,1]$ measures progress along this interpolation, not physical time.

\paragraph{Geometry self-conditioning.}
We retain FlowMol3's coordinate-shaped output head $D_\theta(X_t,t;c)$ and use
its increment $D_\theta-X_t$ as the predicted velocity, after subtracting the
mean atom velocity to keep coordinates centered. Self-conditioning evaluates the
same network twice. The first prediction $v^{(0)}$ gives an estimated endpoint,
$\widehat X_1=X_t+(1-t)v^{(0)}$. The second pass receives this provisional
geometry as an additional input and predicts $v^{(1)}$. Both passes share weights.

We supervise both velocity predictions:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\rm FM}=\frac12\EE\left[
 \frac{\|v^{(0)}-V^*\|_F^2+\|v^{(1)}-V^*\|_F^2}{3N}\right].
\end{equation}
The expectation averages over reference structures, source samples, and training
times. The loss averages the two passes and the $3N$ coordinate errors, giving
each molecule equal weight. Gradients pass through both evaluations. Atom types
and charge inputs remain fixed; edge inputs always indicate ``no bond.'' All
FlowMol3 source comparisons use this same coordinate-only objective and two-pass
architecture.

\paragraph{Sampling.}
We use 32 midpoint integration steps, with two velocity evaluations per step. In
the implemented scheme, the first evaluation uses the current coordinates and
the interval's midpoint time. The second uses the resulting half-step coordinates
at that same time. With two self-conditioning passes per velocity evaluation,
this requires $32\times2\times2=128$ network forward passes per attempted sample.
We then add 0.025\,\AA\ centered Gaussian noise. All FlowMol3 source comparisons
use this sampler, with no subsequent energy-based selection or geometry
optimization. Reported metrics use these raw outputs.

\subsection{Learning physical changes to predicted endpoints}\label{sec:endpoint-correction}
A physical force is defined on a geometry, while a generator predicts a velocity
or a noise vector. We connect these quantities through the generator's
\emph{provisional endpoint}: its current estimate of the final coordinates.
The backbone remains fixed, and a small network learns how physical feedback
should change this estimate (Figure~\ref{fig:method}).

\paragraph{From forces to a regression target.}
Let $X$ be the current coordinates and $p$ the fraction of the generation
schedule completed. For a flow, $p$ is its interpolation time and the endpoint
estimate is $H=X+(1-p)v$, where $v$ is the parent velocity. We evaluate the
centered, mirror-averaged eSEN force $F_+(H)$ during training and form
\begin{equation}
 \delta(H)=\frac{\sigma_H^2}{k_BT_{\rm phys}}F_+(H),
 \qquad T_{\rm phys}=300\ {\rm K}.
 \label{eq:local-force-shift}
\end{equation}
Here $\sigma_H$ is a local coordinate width, distinct from diffusion noise.
Under a first-order energy approximation, multiplying a Gaussian centered at
$H$ by a Boltzmann factor shifts its mean by exactly $\delta(H)$.
We choose $\sigma_H\leq0.03$\,\AA\ so the molecular displacement norm is at
most 0.1\,\AA. Appendix~\ref{app:local-work} gives the derivation.
This local construction supplies a learning target; it does not assume the
parent samples an equilibrium molecular distribution.

The correction network $A_\phi(X,H,p;c)$ receives current and predicted final
coordinates, atom identities, and progress. Symmetric pair coefficients multiply
opposite relative vectors, giving an equivariant correction with zero total
velocity. Its per-atom norm is bounded by $2p^2$\,\AA\ per unit progress.
We fit $A_\phi$ by squared error to $\delta(H)/(1-p)$, uniformly rescaling
this target if necessary to satisfy the same bound. All recorded training
states are retained, including invalid provisional geometries. The final layer
starts at zero, so the initial model reproduces the parent. Only the small
correction network is trained; no gradients update the backbone.

\paragraph{The same endpoint change for flow and diffusion.}
A strength $\eta$ sets the intended endpoint change to $\eta(1-p)A_\phi$.
For diffusion, the parent predicts noise $\epsilon$ and its endpoint estimate
is $H=(X-\sigma_t\epsilon)/\alpha_t$, where $\alpha_t$ and $\sigma_t$ are the
fixed schedule's signal and noise scales. These two parameterizations require
\begin{equation}
 v^{\rm corr}=v+\eta A_\phi,
 \qquad
 \epsilon^{\rm corr}=\epsilon-
       \eta\frac{\alpha_t(1-p)}{\sigma_t}A_\phi.
 \label{eq:endpoint-native-updates}
\end{equation}
Substitution into either endpoint estimate gives the same displacement
$\eta(1-p)A_\phi$. In GAGA, $p=1-t/t_{\max}$, with diffusion noise time $t$
and truncation $t_{\max}=0.65$. The original transitions and observation noise
are retained. Progress is a schedule coordinate, not physical time; the two
samplers need not follow the same dynamics.

The head has 8,178 parameters for FlowMol's atom vocabulary and 7,106 for the
shared EGNN vocabulary. Each fit uses 20,000 updates. Corrected FM sampling
uses 128 backbone and 64 head calls, with no terminal perturbation; the GAGA
comparison uses 128 of each. Neither invokes an energy model during generation.
Appendices~\ref{app:force-correction} and~\ref{app:matched-connection} give the
architecture, bounds, fitting budgets, and strength calibration.

\paragraph{Physical feedback in the backbone weights.}
A complementary implementation fine-tunes two backbone copies on force-shifted
structures and original anchors, then adds the difference between their updates
to the parent. This produces a single adapted network. Appendix~\ref{app:paired-training}
describes the paired update and its controls. We also study work-based weighting
for specified local distributions (Appendices~\ref{app:jarzynski}--\ref{app:rotor-work}).

\paragraph{Conditional hydrogen readout.}
We optionally follow generation with a short flow over hydrogen coordinates,
conditioned on the generated heavy-atom geometry. It learns from reference
structures with perturbed hydrogens. A geometric contact test activates the
readout only when a hydrogen is detached; four midpoint steps then move the
hydrogens together while preserving relative heavy-atom positions. This adds
eight small-network calls per activated output. Appendix~\ref{app:hydrogen-readout}
compares it with a fixed radial attachment rule and a detached-hydrogen-only flow.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
\subsection{Experimental setup}\label{sec:experimental-setup}
We study molecular generation conditioned on atomic composition, total charge,
and spin multiplicity. The main comparison uses an EGNN backbone trained on
20,000 OMol25 structures and evaluates 64 unseen neutral organic singlet
compositions with 17--40 atoms. Each trained model generates 16 structures per
composition. We compare Gaussian-source FM, EDM \citep{edm}, GAGA
\citep{qu2026gaga}, and LatentSpring. The latter combines the harmonic-mixture
source with learned physical correction; the optional hydrogen readout is
excluded from the main comparison and component ablation.

We report two measures. \emph{Graph validity} is the fraction of outputs for
which bond inference produces a connected, sanitized chemical graph with the
requested charge. \emph{Joint yield} additionally requires a GFN2-xTB atomic
force RMS of at most 5 eV/\AA. Both denominators include every generation
attempt. All physical evaluations use the generated coordinates, with no
geometry optimization or energy-based filtering. Confidence intervals resample
compositions and, where specified, fitted models. Appendix~\ref{app:main-implementation}
gives implementation details and Appendix~\ref{app:data-evaluation} specifies the
structural checks.

\section{Discussion}
LatentSpring combines a composition-conditioned harmonic source with physical
corrections learned in a shared coordinate space. The correction improves both
flow and diffusion generators and remains effective when its weights are
transferred between them. Variation across trained parents remains substantial;
the results establish the correction's benefit without a stable ranking between
the two augmented generators. The experiments concern unoptimized neutral organic
structures and approximate energy models. Work-identity tests recover specified
local distributions; global molecular equilibrium remains open.

\section{Normalization of the harmonic mixture}\label{app:proof}
We first compute the density for one tree, then average over trees. The key is
the change of coordinates from independent edge vectors to centered atom positions.
For a tree $T$, let $B_T\in\RR^{(N-1)\times N}$ be its edge-incidence matrix.
Each row has $+1$ and $-1$ at the endpoints of one edge, so $B_TX$ contains the
edge displacements. Let $U\in\RR^{N\times(N-1)}$ have orthonormal columns that
span the centered subspace for one spatial dimension.

Because $B_T\mathbf1=0$, we have
$(B_TU)(B_TU)^\top=B_TB_T^\top$. By Cauchy--Binet,
$\det(B_TB_T^\top)$ is the sum of the squares of its $N$ maximal minors. Every
maximal minor of a tree incidence matrix is $\pm1$, so this determinant is $N$.
The absolute Jacobian is therefore $\sqrt N$ in one spatial dimension and
$N^{3/2}$ in three dimensions. The density for a fixed tree is consequently
$q(X\mid T,c)=N^{3/2}\prod_{e\in T}h_e(B_TX)$, with each edge density evaluated
at its corresponding row of $B_TX$. It integrates to one. Averaging these
normalized components with Equation~\eqref{eq:tree-law} gives
Equation~\eqref{eq:source} and preserves normalization.

\section{Source invariance under symmetry coupling}\label{app:symmetry}
Alignment chooses a rotation and atom relabeling using the reference structure.
We show why a subsequent shared random augmentation restores the source marginal,
even when the source is not Gaussian.

Let $G$ be the compact group of proper rotations and permutations of identical
conditioning labels. Assume that $q_0$ is invariant under $G$ and $X\sim q_0$.
The pairing procedure selects $g(X,Y)\in G$ using the target $Y$. Independently
draw $H$ from the uniform, or Haar, distribution on $G$. Conditional on the orbit
of $X$ and the selected $g$, the transformed source $HgX$ has the same uniform
orbit distribution as $HX$, by Haar invariance. An orbit here is the set of
configurations obtained by applying these symmetries to one configuration.
Alignment changes neither the orbit nor its probability. Averaging over source
orbits therefore recovers $q_0$.

The paired endpoints can still be correlated. The training objective uses their
conditional displacement target directly; this argument establishes the source
marginal, not independence of the endpoints.

\section{Covariance controls}
The shell-tree control uses the same radius-based edge scale but concentrates
edge lengths around it. For each edge, it samples a length
$R=\ell\exp(sZ-s^2/2)$, where $Z\sim N(0,1)$ and $s=0.2$, then samples a
uniform direction. Since $\EE R^2=\ell^2\exp(s^2)$ and the direction has second
moment $I/3$, the edge covariance is $vI=\ell^2\exp(s^2)I/3$.

Let $A_T$ map edge coordinates to centered atom coordinates. Independence of
edges gives $C_T=A_T\operatorname{diag}(v_e)A_T^\top$, where $C_T$ is the
atom-position covariance for one spatial axis and the diagonal matrix contains
edge variances. Replacing the shell vectors by independent Gaussians with
covariance $v_eI$ leaves $C_T$ unchanged. All tree components have zero mean,
so the mixture covariance $\EE_TC_T$ is also unchanged.

The single-Gaussian control estimates this mixture covariance by averaging a
finite number of tree covariances, with randomness seeded by composition. We then
average over permutations within each atom species. This preserves positive
definiteness on the centered subspace and makes the covariance invariant to
relabeling identical atoms. The resulting Gaussian is exactly normalized for its
chosen covariance, but the covariance only approximates that of the full tree
mixture.

\section{Jarzynski identity for escorted transport}\label{app:jarzynski}
This appendix states the change-of-variables identity behind our work-corrected
local targets. Jarzynski's relation connects exponential work averages to
free-energy differences, including in single-molecule pulling
\citep{jarzynski1997master,hummerszabo2001}. Escorted transport adds an imposed
coordinate flow and accounts for its volume change \citep{escorted2008}. Here,
``work'' denotes the corrected energy-like quantity whose exponential supplies an
importance weight.

Fix a composition and use volume measure on its centered coordinate space. Let
$x$ collect the independent coordinates and let
$\beta=1/(k_B T_{\rm phys})$. The unnormalized target is
$\rho_1(x)=\mathbf1_\Omega(x)\exp[-\beta E(x)]$, with finite, nonzero
$Z_1=\int_{\HH_N}\rho_1(x)\,dx$. The allowed region $\Omega$ can, for example,
confine the molecule so that this integral is finite. The indicator is one inside
this region and zero outside it; the normalized target is $\rho_1/Z_1$.

Let $q_0>0$ be the normalized source in Equation~\eqref{eq:source} and let
$\Phi:\HH_N\to\HH_N$ be a differentiable, invertible map with nonzero intrinsic
Jacobian. Its absolute determinant $|\det D\Phi|$ measures local expansion or
compression in the independent coordinates. For $\Phi(x_0)\in\Omega$, define
\begin{equation}
 W_\Phi(x_0)=E(\Phi(x_0))+
 \beta^{-1}\log q_0(x_0)-
 \beta^{-1}\log|\det D\Phi(x_0)|,
\end{equation}
and set $W_\Phi=+\infty$ outside the allowed region, giving zero weight there.
The source-density and Jacobian terms correct for where the mapped samples came
from. Multiplying the exponential weight by the source density and changing
variables gives
\begin{align}
 \EE_{q_0}[e^{-\beta W_\Phi}]
 &=\int_{\HH_N}\rho_1(\Phi(x_0))|\det D\Phi(x_0)|\,dx_0\\
 &=Z_1.
\end{align}
Equivalently, the auxiliary source energy $E_0=-\beta^{-1}\log q_0$ has
$Z_0=1$ in this measure, yielding a Jarzynski-type normalizer identity.
For an observable $f$, we estimate the target mean with
$\sum_j w_j f(\Phi(x_j))/\sum_j w_j$, where $w_j=e^{-\beta W_\Phi(x_j)}$.
This is self-normalized importance sampling. Its finite-sample variance depends
on how well the mapped proposals cover the target.

For an exact, sufficiently regular ordinary differential equation (ODE) flow,
the intrinsic log determinant equals the integrated velocity divergence.
Our implemented work corrections instead use explicit maps with tractable local
densities: Gaussian translations in Appendix~\ref{app:local-work} and periodic
rotor maps with analytic Jacobians in Appendix~\ref{app:rotor-work}. We do not
use an exact neural-flow likelihood to evaluate the reported generator. Its
outputs use the midpoint solver and terminal noise in Section~\ref{sec:flow}.
