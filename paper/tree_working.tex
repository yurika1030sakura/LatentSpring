\begin{abstract}
Generating three-dimensional molecules from atomic composition requires learning both connectivity and geometry. We present LatentSpring, a flow-matching model that combines a harmonic-mixture source with learned geometric and physical corrections. The source samples atom positions along random auxiliary trees using element-dependent length scales, without requiring a chemical bond graph. Recovery training teaches the flow to restore perturbed geometries, while two small equivariant networks refine its predicted endpoints using reference structures and force feedback. On 64 unseen compositions, LatentSpring achieves a graph-and-force yield of 35.35\% across five fits, compared with 8.75\% for GAGA and 8.15\% for EDM, at a GFN2-xTB force-RMS threshold of 5 eV/\AA. Adding local geometry checks gives a yield of 9.63\%, compared with 4.43\% for the initial force-corrected flow. This improvement holds in all three additional training repetitions, and the complete method outperforms a continuation control with matched backbone updates. The endpoint formulation also allows the trained correction networks to improve EDM and GAGA without retraining. Sampling requires only neural-network evaluations, with no energy queries or geometry optimization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Molecular generation from atomic composition requires a model to determine how the atoms connect and how they are arranged in three dimensions. Unlike graph-conditioned conformer generation, the input provides no chemical connectivity. We study this task conditioned on atom identities, total charge, and spin multiplicity, generating coordinates from which bonds are subsequently inferred for evaluation.

Flow matching transports samples from a source distribution to a target distribution \citep{lipman2023flowmatching}. The usual isotropic Gaussian source treats all atoms on a common spatial scale. Harmonic sources introduce geometric correlations through virtual springs, but existing graph-conditioned methods construct these springs from known bonds. Composition-conditioned generation therefore calls for a structured source that does not rely on an input molecular graph.

LatentSpring addresses this problem with a mixture of random auxiliary trees. Each tree supplies independent Gaussian edge displacements whose scales depend on the atoms' covalent radii. Adding these displacements along tree paths gives the initial coordinates; averaging over trees defines a normalized harmonic mixture. The tree is discarded after initialization, leaving the flow free to generate a different connectivity, including rings. An equivariant velocity network with geometry self-conditioning then transforms the source into molecular coordinates.

A structured source alone does not ensure accurate local geometry. In our experiments, many outputs pass chemical-graph checks and a residual-force threshold yet retain distorted local arrangements. We address these errors through geometric recovery training and two endpoint-correction networks. Recovery training teaches the backbone to restore perturbed reference geometries. A geometric network then corrects the predicted endpoint, followed by a physical network trained on force feedback. Learning the force correction offline avoids evaluating an atomic potential at every sampling step.

Both networks operate on the generator's predicted final coordinates rather than its internal features. Their coordinate displacements can therefore be converted into either a flow-velocity correction or a diffusion-noise correction. We test this interface by directly reusing FM-trained correction weights in EDM and GAGA.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A normalized harmonic-mixture source that introduces element-dependent geometric correlations without requiring chemical bonds. We derive its density and compare it with isotropic, shell-tree, and covariance-matched sources.
\item A geometric--physical refinement method that combines recovery training with two small equivariant endpoint-correction networks. A common coordinate interface applies the learned corrections to flow matching and diffusion.
\item Improved raw molecular geometry across five fits, with gains in all three additional training repetitions and over a continuation control with matched backbone updates. The correction networks also improve EDM and GAGA without target-specific retraining.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Molecular flows and source distributions.}
ConfGF and GeoDiff generate conformations conditioned on a chemical graph \citep{shi2021confgf,xu2022geodiff}. HarmonicFlow combines harmonic sources with self-conditioned flow matching for ligand docking, and FlowSite extends the approach to binding-site design \citep{stark2024harmonicflow}. ET-Flow likewise constructs its harmonic source from a supplied molecular graph \citep{etflow}. Our source instead averages over auxiliary trees conditioned only on atomic composition. Other source-adaptation methods guide a frozen vector field \citep{wang2026sourceguided} or learn a condition-dependent source \citep{kim2026bettersource}. Our main experiments use the EGNN underlying EDM \citep{edm}; the earlier source experiments use FlowMol3's coordinate network and self-conditioning modules \citep{flowmol3}.

\paragraph{Distributions over trees.}
Factored tree distributions support tractable probabilities and learning \citep{meila2000trees,duan2023spanning}. Random spanning-tree differences have also been used to define Gaussian mixtures for imaging inverse problems \citep{everink2026random}. We use isotropic edge displacements with atomic length scales to define a distribution on centered molecular coordinates. Isotropic Gaussian, shell-tree, and covariance-matched Gaussian controls test the effect of this source construction.

\paragraph{Physical feedback and nonequilibrium work.}
OMol25 provides molecular structures, energies, and forces across electronic conditions \citep{omol25}. Physical information can enter generative training through energy-weighted flow matching \citep{dern2025energyweighted}, importance sampling \citep{fab}, physical-reward fine-tuning \citep{zhou2025rlpf}, or simulation-based protein fine-tuning \citep{lu2024fusing}. Residual physical correction has also been studied for frozen video flow fields \citep{wang2026pila}. In molecular modeling, FLEXDOCK regularizes predicted docking endpoints with a geometric potential \citep{corso2025flexdock}; ConfDiff learns force guidance for protein conformations \citep{wang2024confdiff}; and \citet{lai2026forceguidance} apply MMFF94 guidance during flow and diffusion sampling. Our physical network learns bounded coordinate corrections from forces at generated endpoints while the backbone is fixed.

We also examine local target reweighting through nonequilibrium work identities \citep{jarzynski1997master,hummerszabo2001,escorted2008,jarzynski2002targeted}. Learned escorting has been used for multistate free-energy estimation \citep{holdijk2026escorted}. Our supplementary studies test local distribution recovery on controlled molecular rotors and its effect on subsequent neural training.

\section{A harmonic source with latent connectivity}
The condition $c=((z_i)_{i=1}^{N},Q,S)$ specifies each atom's atomic number $z_i$, the total charge $Q$ in elementary-charge units, and spin multiplicity $S$. For example, neutral singlet $\mathrm{C_2H_6O}$ has $Q=0$ and $S=1$, but permits several connectivities. Chemical bonds are inferred for evaluation and are not training labels.

The output is a coordinate matrix $X\in\RR^{N\times3}$, with positions $x_i$ in angstroms. Subtracting the mean position removes translation and places $X$ in the $3(N-1)$-dimensional centered space $\HH_N=\{X:\sum_i x_i=0\}$.

\paragraph{Tree distribution.}
We sample a spanning tree $T$ over the atoms, assigning each possible edge a positive symmetric affinity $a_{ij}=a_{ji}$. The probability of a tree is proportional to the product of its affinities:
\begin{equation}
 p(T\mid c)=\frac{\prod_{(i,j)\in T}a_{ij}}{\tau(a)},\qquad
 \tau(a)=\sum_T\prod_{(i,j)\in T}a_{ij}.
 \label{eq:tree-law}
\end{equation}
Here $\tau(a)$ sums the weights of all spanning trees. We use $a_{ij}=u_i u_j$, with $u_i=0.05$ for H and F/Cl/Br/I and $u_i=\max(1,\text{default valence}(z_i)-1)$ otherwise. Higher values favor more incident edges, while the small hydrogen and halogen weights favor terminal positions. These affinities use element-level valences rather than molecule-specific connectivity.

\paragraph{Coordinate sampling.}
Each edge has an independent Gaussian displacement $d\in\RR^3$. Let $\ell_{ij}$ be the sum of the two atoms' covalent radii and set $\sigma_{ij}^2=\ell_{ij}^2\exp(s^2)/3$, with $s=0.2$. The edge density is
\begin{equation}
 h_{ij}(d)=(2\pi\sigma_{ij}^2)^{-3/2}
 \exp\!\left[-\frac{\|d\|^2}{2\sigma_{ij}^2}\right].
 \label{eq:edge-law}
\end{equation}
Thus $\sigma_{ij}$ is the standard deviation along each axis, and the covariance matches that of the lognormal shell control. We add edge vectors along tree paths and center the resulting coordinates. The FlowMol implementation uses weighted Wilson walks; the shared EGNN uses weighted Pr\"ufer sequences to sample the same factorized tree law. Drawing a new tree for each sample yields a source invariant to rotations and permutations of identical atoms.

\paragraph{Normalized mixture density.}
Marginalizing the auxiliary tree gives a mixture of harmonic networks:
\begin{proposition}[Normalized source density]
For normalized isotropic edge densities,
\begin{equation}
 q_0(X\mid c)=N^{3/2}
 \frac{\tau\big(a_{ij}h_{ij}(x_i-x_j)\big)}{\tau(a)}
 \label{eq:source}
\end{equation}
integrates to one over the centered coordinate space $\HH_N$.
\end{proposition}
The numerator weights each tree edge by its displacement density. The Jacobian factor $N^{3/2}$ converts edge vectors to centered atom coordinates. Both tree sums can be evaluated by the matrix-tree theorem; Appendix~\ref{app:proof} derives the normalization. Training requires only the tree-and-edge sampler, not determinant evaluation. The Gaussian edge densities correspond to quadratic spring energies, hence the term \emph{harmonic source}.

\section{Integration with molecular flow matching}\label{sec:flow}
Starting from $X_0\sim q_0(\cdot\mid c)$, we integrate $v_\theta(X_t,t;c)\in\RR^{N\times3}$ from $t=0$ to $t=1$ to obtain $X_{\rm gen}\in\HH_N$. The network receives current coordinates, a time embedding, and encoded atom identities. The experiments fix charge $Q=0$ and spin multiplicity $S=1$; the sampled tree serves only to initialize $X_0$.

\paragraph{Training pairs.}
We pair a source sample $X_0$ with an OMol25 reference $X_1$, matching atoms of the same type and aligning the coordinates by rotation. A shared uniform random rotation and random permutation of identical atoms then preserve the source marginal (Appendix~\ref{app:symmetry}). Along the linear interpolation $X_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1$, the regression target is $V^*=X_1-X_0$. Here $t\in[0,1]$ denotes interpolation progress.

\paragraph{Geometry self-conditioning.}
The EGNN is evaluated twice with shared weights. Its first velocity prediction $v^{(0)}$ defines a provisional endpoint $\widehat X_1=X_t+(1-t)v^{(0)}$. Pairwise distances from this detached geometry enter an invariant edge channel in the second pass, which predicts $v^{(1)}$. Both predictions are supervised:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\rm FM}=\frac12\EE\left[
 \frac{\|v^{(0)}-V^*\|_F^2+\|v^{(1)}-V^*\|_F^2}{3N}\right].
\end{equation}
The expectation is over reference structures, source samples, and interpolation times. Normalization by $3N$ gives each molecule equal weight. Gradients train both passes but do not propagate through the distance feedback. The one-pass ablation omits the second evaluation; Appendix~\ref{app:source-study-setup} describes the earlier FlowMol implementation.

\paragraph{Geometric recovery training.}
To improve recovery from sampling errors, we continue backbone training on perturbed late interpolants. Perturbations add local atom noise or displace a spatial region as a group. The recovery objective compares the predicted endpoint coordinates, local distances, and angles with the original reference, alternating with the FM loss. Supervision uses coordinates and element identities. Training-time geometric distortion has also benefited molecular flow models \citep{flowmol3}. Appendix~\ref{app:geometric-recovery} compares the complete recovery procedure with unperturbed endpoint continuation using the same number of backbone updates.

\paragraph{Sampling.}
We use 32 midpoint steps. Each step evaluates the velocity at the current state and again at the half-step state; two self-conditioning passes per evaluation give $32\times2\times2=128$ backbone calls. Applying the two endpoint corrections below adds 64 calls to each small network. We return the raw coordinates without terminal noise, energy-based selection, or geometry optimization.

\subsection{Geometric and physical endpoint corrections}\label{sec:endpoint-correction}
We train separate networks for geometric recovery and force-based refinement (Figure~\ref{fig:method}). Both act on the provisional endpoint: the generator's current prediction of its final coordinates. For a flow with current state $X$, velocity $v$, and schedule progress $p=t$, this estimate is $H=X+(1-p)v$.

\paragraph{Geometric correction.}
For a reference $X_{\rm ref}$ that passes the geometric checks, we construct a perturbed endpoint $\widetilde H$ and train $G_\psi(\widetilde H,p;c)$ toward $(X_{\rm ref}-\widetilde H)/(1-p)$. Uniform rescaling caps each atom's target norm at $4p^2$\,\AA\ per unit progress; unperturbed references receive zero targets. The network uses element identities, pair distances, local coordination, and neighboring element embeddings. Symmetric pair features and opposite vector messages ensure rotation/permutation equivariance and zero total velocity. Appendix~\ref{app:geometric-recovery} gives the reference screen, perturbations, and architecture.

\paragraph{Physical correction.}
We collect predicted endpoints from the initial flow and evaluate their centered, mirror-averaged eSEN forces $F_+(H)$. The target coordinate shift is
\begin{equation}
 \delta(H)=\frac{\sigma_H^2}{k_BT_{\rm phys}}F_+(H),
 \qquad T_{\rm phys}=300\ {\rm K}.
 \label{eq:local-force-shift}
\end{equation}
Under a first-order energy approximation, Boltzmann reweighting of a Gaussian centered at $H$ shifts its mean by $\delta(H)$. We choose its local width $\sigma_H\leq0.03$\,\AA\ to keep the molecular displacement norm within 0.1\,\AA\ (Appendix~\ref{app:local-work}). This local width is independent of the diffusion-noise scale.

The physical network $A_\phi(X,H,p;c)$ predicts a velocity correction from the current coordinates, endpoint estimate, atom identities, and progress. As in the geometric network, symmetric coefficients and opposite pair vectors give an equivariant, zero-sum field. We minimize squared error to $\delta(H)/(1-p)$, with both targets and predictions bounded by $2p^2$\,\AA\ per atom per unit progress. Training includes every recorded state, including invalid endpoints, and initializes the final layer to zero. The backbone is fixed during this stage. We subsequently reuse the physical weights with the geometrically refined backbone and in transfer experiments.

\paragraph{Sequential endpoint refinement.}
The geometric network predicts $g=G_\psi(H,p;c)$, giving $H_g=H+(1-p)g$. The physical network then evaluates $f=A_\phi(X,H_g,p;c)$. With physical strength $\eta$, the total endpoint displacement is $(1-p)(g+\eta f)$.

For a diffusion model, $H=(X-\sigma_t\epsilon)/\alpha_t$, where $\epsilon$ is the predicted noise and $\alpha_t,\sigma_t$ are the schedule's signal and noise scales. The corresponding native-output corrections are
\begin{equation}
 v^{\rm corr}=v+g+\eta f,
 \qquad
 \epsilon^{\rm corr}=\epsilon-
       \frac{\alpha_t(1-p)}{\sigma_t}(g+\eta f).
 \label{eq:endpoint-native-updates}
\end{equation}
Both produce the specified endpoint displacement. GAGA uses $p=1-t/t_{\max}$ with $t_{\max}=0.65$. Transfer preserves the target's source distribution, reverse transitions, and final observation noise. All sampling-time corrections are neural evaluations.

Appendices~\ref{app:force-correction} and~\ref{app:matched-connection} detail the architectures, bounds, and fitting protocol. Separate studies examine paired backbone updates (Appendix~\ref{app:paired-training}) and work-weighted local targets (Appendix~\ref{app:jarzynski}).

\paragraph{Optional hydrogen readout.}
A conditional flow trained on perturbed reference hydrogens adjusts hydrogen coordinates while preserving relative heavy-atom positions. A detached-hydrogen contact test activates the readout, which uses four midpoint steps and eight network calls (Appendix~\ref{app:hydrogen-readout}).

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
\subsection{Experimental setup}\label{sec:experimental-setup}
We train the main EGNN on 20,000 OMol25 structures and evaluate 64 unseen neutral organic singlet compositions with 17--40 atoms. Each fit generates 16 structures per composition. The comparison includes Gaussian-source FM, EDM \citep{edm}, GAGA \citep{qu2026gaga}, and LatentSpring, which combines the harmonic source, geometric recovery, and endpoint corrections. The main comparison and component ablation exclude the optional hydrogen readout.

We report three nested criteria. \emph{Graph validity} requires an inferred connected, sanitized chemical graph with the requested total charge. \emph{Graph-and-force yield}, abbreviated \emph{joint yield}, additionally requires GFN2-xTB atomic force RMS at most 5 eV/\AA. \emph{Geometry-and-force yield} also requires zero assigned radicals and the specified bond-length, angle, internal-distance, and planarity checks. Every rate uses all generation attempts as its denominator, and all structures are evaluated at their raw coordinates. Confidence intervals resample compositions and, where stated, fitted models. Appendices~\ref{app:main-implementation} and~\ref{app:data-evaluation} give implementation and evaluation details.

\section{Discussion}
LatentSpring combines an element-aware harmonic source with learned geometric and physical endpoint corrections. Across five fits, the complete method improves raw graph-and-force and geometry-and-force yields; it also outperforms unperturbed endpoint continuation with matched backbone updates. Direct weight reuse in EDM and GAGA shows that endpoint corrections can remain useful across generative objectives within the shared architecture.

Chemical quality remains the main limitation. Fragmentation, nonplanarity, and unusual inferred charge patterns persist even after geometric refinement. Improving global connectivity and charge assignment is therefore necessary for more reliable composition-conditioned generation.

\section{Normalization of the harmonic mixture}\label{app:proof}
For a tree $T$, let $B_T\in\RR^{(N-1)\times N}$ be its oriented edge-incidence matrix, so that $B_TX$ contains the edge displacements. Let $U\in\RR^{N\times(N-1)}$ have orthonormal columns spanning the centered subspace in one spatial dimension. We compute the Jacobian from independent edge vectors to centered positions and then average the resulting density over trees.

Since $B_T\mathbf1=0$, $(B_TU)(B_TU)^\top=B_TB_T^\top$. Cauchy--Binet expresses $\det(B_TB_T^\top)$ as the sum of squares of the $N$ maximal minors. Each minor of a tree incidence matrix is $\pm1$, giving determinant $N$ and absolute Jacobian $\sqrt N$ per spatial dimension. The three-dimensional density is therefore $q(X\mid T,c)=N^{3/2}\prod_{e\in T}h_e(B_TX)$, with each factor evaluated at its edge displacement. It integrates to one. Averaging with the tree law in Equation~\eqref{eq:tree-law} gives the normalized mixture in Equation~\eqref{eq:source}.

\section{Source invariance under symmetry coupling}\label{app:symmetry}
The alignment used to pair source and reference structures depends on both endpoints. A shared random symmetry restores the source marginal after this alignment.

Let $G$ be the compact group of proper rotations and permutations of identical conditioning labels, and let $X\sim q_0$ with $q_0$ invariant under $G$. Alignment selects $g(X,Y)\in G$ using the reference $Y$. Draw an independent Haar-uniform $H\in G$. Conditional on the orbit of $X$ and on $g$, Haar invariance gives $HgX$ the same uniform orbit distribution as $HX$. Alignment preserves the orbit, and averaging over source orbits recovers $q_0$. The paired endpoints may remain correlated; the result establishes the marginal required for the conditional-displacement training objective.

\section{Covariance controls}
The shell-tree control samples an edge length $R=\ell\exp(sZ-s^2/2)$, with $Z\sim N(0,1)$ and $s=0.2$, and an independent uniform direction. Since $\EE R^2=\ell^2\exp(s^2)$ and the directional second moment is $I/3$, the edge covariance is $vI=\ell^2\exp(s^2)I/3$.

Let $A_T$ map edge vectors to centered atom coordinates. Edge independence gives $C_T=A_T\operatorname{diag}(v_e)A_T^\top$ for the atom-position covariance along one axis. Gaussian edge vectors with covariance $v_eI$ produce the same $C_T$. All component means are zero, so the shell-tree and harmonic mixtures also share covariance $\EE_TC_T$.

The single-Gaussian control approximates this mixture covariance by averaging a finite number of tree covariances, seeded by composition, and then averaging over permutations within each element. This construction preserves positive definiteness on the centered subspace and invariance to identical-atom relabeling. The Gaussian is normalized exactly for the resulting estimated covariance.

\section{Jarzynski identity for escorted transport}\label{app:jarzynski}
The work correction follows from a change-of-variables identity. Jarzynski's relation links exponential work averages to free-energy differences \citep{jarzynski1997master,hummerszabo2001}; escorted transport includes an imposed coordinate map and its volume change \citep{escorted2008}.

Fix a composition and use intrinsic volume measure on its centered coordinate space. Let $x$ denote independent coordinates and $\beta=1/(k_B T_{\rm phys})$. Consider $\rho_1(x)=\mathbf1_\Omega(x)\exp[-\beta E(x)]$ with finite, nonzero $Z_1=\int_{\HH_N}\rho_1(x)\,dx$. The region $\Omega$ specifies the allowed configurations and can provide the confinement needed for a finite normalizer. The target density is $\rho_1/Z_1$.

Let $q_0>0$ be the normalized source in Equation~\eqref{eq:source}, and let $\Phi:\HH_N\to\HH_N$ be differentiable and invertible with nonzero intrinsic Jacobian. For $\Phi(x_0)\in\Omega$, define
\begin{equation}
 W_\Phi(x_0)=E(\Phi(x_0))+
 \beta^{-1}\log q_0(x_0)-
 \beta^{-1}\log|\det D\Phi(x_0)|,
\end{equation}
Set $W_\Phi=+\infty$ outside the allowed region. The source-density and Jacobian terms account for the proposal distribution and local volume change. Changing variables gives
\begin{align}
 \EE_{q_0}[e^{-\beta W_\Phi}]
 &=\int_{\HH_N}\rho_1(\Phi(x_0))|\det D\Phi(x_0)|\,dx_0\\
 &=Z_1.
\end{align}
The auxiliary source energy $E_0=-\beta^{-1}\log q_0$ has $Z_0=1$, yielding the Jarzynski-type normalizer identity. For an observable $f$, self-normalized importance sampling estimates its target mean by $\sum_j w_j f(\Phi(x_j))/\sum_j w_j$, with $w_j=e^{-\beta W_\Phi(x_j)}$. Its finite-sample variance depends on proposal coverage of the target.

For a sufficiently regular exact ODE flow, the intrinsic log determinant is the integrated velocity divergence. The implemented local-work studies instead use Gaussian translations (Appendix~\ref{app:local-work}) and periodic rotor maps with analytic Jacobians (Appendix~\ref{app:rotor-work}). They evaluate the densities of these explicit local maps, not an exact likelihood of the discretized neural generator.
